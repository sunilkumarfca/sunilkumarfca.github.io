Nonprofit Reserves Policy: How Many Months of Reserves Is Enough?

Part of the Institutional Governance & Resilience section of the Knowledge Hub.

Part of the guide: How funders and CSR partners check an NGO's governance

By CA Sunil Kumar, FCA · 17 November 2026 · 4 min read

A reserves policy answers one question for the Board: if our income stopped or fell sharply tomorrow, how long could we keep our promises? Most NGOs only find out the answer when a major grant ends. A written policy lets the Board decide it in advance.

This page is for Board members, treasurers, CEOs and finance heads of nonprofits, NGOs and charities anywhere: a nonprofit board in the US, charity trustees in the UK and Europe, or an NGO board in Kenya, Nigeria, Uganda, South Africa or India.

General guidance for governance and education. Check the rules of your regulator, your constitution and your donors, which can limit what you may hold.

The short answer

Many Boards start with a range of three to six months of operating costs. That is a starting point, not a rule. An NGO that depends on one large donor, has long staff notice periods or owns a building with running costs may need more. An organisation with many small donors and costs it can cut quickly may need less. The right number comes from your own risks, worked out in the way shown below.

What counts as reserves

ItemCounts as reserves?Why
Unrestricted cash and investmentsYesThe Board is free to spend it
Funds the Board has set aside (designated) for a planUsually shown separatelyThe Board can undo the designation, but it has a purpose
Restricted grants and donor-designated fundsNoThey belong to a donor's purpose
Buildings, vehicles and equipment in useNoYou cannot spend them without selling them
Money owed by donors that has not arrivedWith careCount it only if it is certain and close

The most common mistake is to count the bank balance. A large balance can be almost all restricted money, which means real reserves are close to zero.

How to work out how many months you need

Instead of picking a number, list what would have to be paid if income stopped, and how long it would take to respond. Four building blocks cover most NGOs:

  1. Time to replace lost income. How many months would it realistically take to find a new donor or cut programmes in an orderly way?
  2. Commitments you cannot walk away from. Staff notice periods and end-of-service benefits, office leases, contracts with partners and suppliers.
  3. Timing gaps. Donors who pay in arrears or reimburse after spending. The gap between paying staff and receiving the grant instalment has to be funded from somewhere.
  4. Shocks. A disallowed cost from a donor audit, a currency movement, a regulatory delay that freezes a bank account, or an emergency the mission cannot ignore.

A simple worked example

An NGO spends 100,000 a month (any currency). Its largest donor provides 45% of income.

Building blockEstimateMonths of cost
Time to replace or scale down the largest grant4 months of the 45% share1.8
Staff notice and end-of-service costs, lease exitCalculated from contracts1.5
Reimbursement timing gap on donor grantsAbout 6 weeks of grant-funded spend1.0

Target5 months (about 500,000)

The Board might then set a target range of four to six months, with the lower end as the point that triggers action. The exact figures matter less than the fact that each one can be explained to an auditor, a donor or a new Board member.

What the reserves policy should say

  • Why the organisation holds reserves, in one or two sentences.
  • What counts as reserves, using the definitions above.
  • The target range, in months of operating costs and in money, and how it was calculated.
  • What happens if reserves fall below the range: a recovery plan, how quickly, and who reports on it.
  • What happens if reserves rise above the range: how the extra will be used for the mission.
  • Who may use reserves, for what, and what needs Board approval.
  • Where reserves are held and how they are invested (low risk, easy to reach).
  • How often the policy is reviewed. Once a year, with the budget, is normal.

Five questions for the next Board meeting

  1. What are our unrestricted reserves today, in months of costs, not in money?
  2. How much of our bank balance is restricted?
  3. If our largest donor stopped tomorrow, what would we do in the first 90 days?
  4. Are we above, inside or below our target range, and has that changed since the last meeting?
  5. Is the reserves figure explained clearly in our annual report?

Regulators and funders increasingly expect this. In England and Wales, for example, trustees are expected to explain their reserves policy in the annual report, and many institutional donors ask about reserves in a pre-award assessment. A short written policy answers both.

Questions people ask

How many months of reserves should a nonprofit have?

There is no single right number. Many Boards use three to six months of operating costs as a starting range, then adjust for their own risks: how concentrated their funding is, how quickly costs can be cut, and what commitments they cannot walk away from. The number should come from a calculation, not a habit.

What counts as reserves for a nonprofit?

Reserves are the unrestricted funds the Board is free to spend, after setting aside money tied up in fixed assets and money already committed. Restricted grants and donor-designated funds are not reserves, even if they sit in the same bank account.

Can a nonprofit have too much in reserves?

Yes. Money held without a clear reason is money not spent on the mission, and donors and regulators may ask why. A good policy states a target range and what the Board will do if reserves rise above it as well as below it.

Who approves the reserves policy?

The Board approves it, usually on the advice of the finance or audit committee, and reviews it at least once a year with the budget. Management reports actual reserves against the target at every Board meeting.
