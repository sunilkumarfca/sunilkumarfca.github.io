Free practice tool for NGOs and organisations · From the book Optimise Your Return on Effort

Where should our money and effort go?

Every organisation has more good uses for money, staff time and leadership attention than it can fund. This free tool applies the SROE (Strategic Return on Effort) framework to real organisational choices. Should we outsource, hire staff or use a consultant? Should we buy software or add people? Which departments should get the next rupee?

For Boards, CEOs, CFOs and department heads. Start with a 2-minute quick check that gives a result straight away; the full deep dive takes about 30 minutes for one decision. Answers stay in this browser on your device; nothing is sent anywhere. Deciding for yourself? Use the individual version.

What a 2-minute check cannot see. It does not separate the different returns (mission impact, quality, compliance, capability built), the full 3-year cost and management time, dependence on one vendor or person, how sure you are, or whether you can exit. The deep dive does, and it also shows which functions should get the next rupee.

This is an indicative result, not a recommendation. It turns your own answers into a simple comparison to help you think. It does not decide for you.

How the score works, and why

Value = good it does × 50% + fit × 30% + future choices × 20%. The good it does carries the most weight because it is the reason to act at all. Fit carries 30% because value that does not serve what matters now is often wasted. Future choices (whether an option opens or closes doors later) carry 20% because they matter but are harder to judge. In this quick check, future choices are fixed at the middle score (3) for every option, so the quick check does not compare them. The deep dive asks about them.

Effort and risk = what it takes × 60% + how bad the downside would be × 40%. What it takes is certain; the downside may never happen, so it counts a little less.

Value for effort = value ÷ effort and risk. A result of 1.0 means you get back about what you put in. High = 1.3 or more (at least 30% more value than effort and risk) with at least a partial fit. Low = below 0.9 (it takes more than it gives), or a downside the organisation could not cope with. In between is Medium: promising but uncertain.

Total value matters too. Value for effort shows the return on each unit of effort. An option with a lower ratio can still create more value in total. The result tells you when this happens.

What the answers mean
  • Good it does and what it takes: Very little = hardly noticeable; A little = a small difference; Some = a clear difference; A lot = a big difference; A great deal = a major change.
  • Fit: Poorly = works against what matters now; Partly = helps one priority and is neutral for the rest; Perfectly = directly serves the top priority.
  • How bad the downside would be: Minor = easily absorbed; Moderate = a setback that takes months to recover from; Severe = lasting damage to health, money, reputation or relationships. Think of how likely it is and how much it would hurt together.
  • Could the organisation cope? Yes means the worst case could be absorbed without lasting damage.

When to take the deep dive: if the options are close (within about 20%), if the result is Medium, if the decision is large or hard to reverse, or if it needs Board or donor approval. The deep dive separates each kind of return and cost, checks exit and dependence, and shows where the next rupee should go.

Step 2: SROE decision test

Does the complete exchange make sense for the organisation? Look at what each option creates, what it costs over time, what it displaces and what happens if it goes wrong. Keep "do nothing" or "delay" as one option.

How to rate returns, costs and risk

Financial returns (cost saving, income, cost avoided): estimate the money over three years and compare it with the option's full cost. Some = it roughly pays for itself; A lot = it returns clearly more than it costs; A great deal = several times its cost.

Non-financial returns (mission impact, quality, compliance, capability, donor confidence): use evidence where you have it, such as MEL data on people reached, audit findings, donor feedback and staff time saved. Some = a clear, visible improvement; A lot = a change the Board and donors would notice; A great deal = a step change in that area. Count returns that come later, such as capability built inside the organisation, at their value over time.

Costs: include the full cost over the period: fees or salaries, management time, setup, training and disruption, not only the price.

How bad the downside would be: think of how likely it is and how much it would hurt: money lost, compliance breaches, damage to beneficiaries, donors or reputation. Something likely and painful is Serious or Severe.

Can we get out of it later: check notice periods, contract exit terms, data ownership and handover.

Could the organisation cope: could reserves, other funding or a fallback plan absorb the worst case without harming programmes or compliance?

Scoring settings (you may change)

Value = Return × + Strategic fit × + Optionality ×

Cost = Effort × + Risk ×

High profile if ratio at least (and fit 3+); Low if below .

Return score = average of the option's three biggest returns. Risk score = average of downside and uncertainty, plus 0.5 if exit is possible only with some cost, or 1 if there is no practical exit (maximum 5). Any option whose downside the organisation could not survive is marked Low. Changing the decision type resets these to that type's suggested settings.

Why these weights: outsourcing, hiring and consultant choices weigh cost and risk equally (50/50) because each is a long commitment with real exit costs. Software and ERP choices give future options more weight (30%) because systems shape what the organisation can do for years. Other decisions use the general setting: value 50/30/20, cost 60/40. High needs a ratio of 1.3 or more (clearly more value than cost); Low is below 0.9 (it costs more than it gives). Change them if your context is different.

This is a decision aid, not a mathematical answer. It makes the full exchange visible for management and the Board; the judgement stays with you.

Step 3: Fund allocation across functions

Where should the next rupee (or dollar) go? Importance and strength come from Step 1. Add this year's budget for each function, the value of extra funding there, and the risk if it is not funded. Use any one currency.

Extra funding needed = the additional amount next year, beyond this year's budget, that would close the gap in that function. Fund the "Strengthen first" functions first. Ask donors for the rest as specific, costed requests, or plan it over more than one year.

Funding priority = Importance × (6 − Strength) × (Value of extra funding + Risk if not funded) ÷ 2. Strengthen first = one of the top three by funding priority. Review spend = extra funding adds little value (2 or less) but the function already takes more than its average share of budget. Protect comes first: statutory compliance, safeguarding and donor obligations need their minimum funding whatever the score.
