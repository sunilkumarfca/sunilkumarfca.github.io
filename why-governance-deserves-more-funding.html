Why Governance Deserves More Funding: Protecting Programme Outcomes and Lasting Impact

Part of the Institutional Governance & Resilience section of the Knowledge Hub.

By CA Sunil Kumar, FCA · 9 November 2026 · 13 min read

Donors expect their NGO partners to have strong governance, effective controls, capable leadership and sound accountability. Yet the resources needed to build and sustain these capabilities are often overlooked. The problem is not simply that governance is underfunded. It is that its contribution to organisational objectives and lasting programme impact is not always recognised.

Programmes cannot deliver lasting outcomes without funded governance. Governance is not a cost competing with programmes. It is part of what every donor, CEO and Board is already trying to achieve. This article sets out why, and proposes a simple way to tell whether it is working: the five-year test.

1. The governance funding paradox

Imagine an NGO implementing programmes in education, healthcare and livelihoods. Its donors expect it to have:

  • An effective and independent Board providing appropriate oversight.
  • Strong financial management and internal controls.
  • Capable leadership and appropriately skilled people.
  • Effective programme management and monitoring systems.
  • Risk management and internal audit arrangements.
  • Safeguarding and beneficiary accountability mechanisms.
  • Appropriate data protection and cybersecurity.
  • Sound compliance, procurement and grant management.
  • Financial sustainability and succession planning.

These are reasonable expectations, and essential for an organisation entrusted with donor resources and responsibility for communities.

But there is an important question: are the resources allocated to establish, operate, monitor and improve these capabilities adequate? Often, they may not be. Many organisations are expected to maintain increasingly sophisticated governance while working with small finance teams, limited systems, partial assurance coverage and little flexible institutional funding.

This creates a contradiction: organisations are expected to demonstrate governance standards that their funding arrangements may not enable them to achieve.

The answer is not expensive systems or large administrative teams for every NGO. Governance should be proportionate to an organisation's size, complexity, funding structure and risk exposure. The question is whether the organisation has the capabilities it genuinely needs to meet its responsibilities.

2. Why governance does not receive adequate priority

One reason is that governance is usually seen as a supporting function, not as part of organisational performance.

Programme expenditure is visible. It can be linked to beneficiaries reached, schools supported, health services delivered and livelihoods created. Governance expenditure is less visible. An effective control may prevent a loss that never appears. A strong safeguarding mechanism may prevent harm. Good risk management may avoid a programme disruption. None of this shows up in conventional programme reporting.

Other reasons reinforce this:

  • Short-term funding orientation. Donors often fund defined projects over fixed periods, while institutional capability needs continuous investment beyond any single grant.
  • A narrow view of governance. Governance is often equated with policies, statutory compliance, audits and administrative cost, rather than with decision-making, programme quality, accountability and effectiveness.
  • Competing priorities. CEOs and programme teams naturally focus on delivery, fundraising and growth. Governance investment is easily postponed when its benefits are not immediately visible.
  • Weak demonstration of value. NGOs themselves do not always explain how governance investment improves programme performance, protects resources and sustains the institution.

Sector research in India points the same way: most NGOs report difficulty in getting their institutional costs covered, and many funders who say they support institutional strengthening are not seen that way by the organisations they fund.

So the problem is not only one of funding. It is also about how funders and NGOs understand, prioritise and demonstrate the value of governance. The responsibility is shared.

3. Governance is part of every stakeholder's primary concern

Governance need not compete with the priorities of donors, CEOs, Boards or programme teams. It may be more useful to see it as part of each of them, often invisible, but integral.

  • Donors want their resources to create meaningful and sustainable outcomes. Governance helps ensure that programmes address genuine needs, resources are used as intended, implementation is monitored and results are properly assessed.
  • CEOs want effective delivery, growth and sustainability. Governance provides the people, processes, systems, information and accountability needed to make sound decisions.
  • Boards want the organisation to fulfil its mission responsibly and remain sustainable. Governance enables oversight, risk management, accountability and strategic direction.
  • Programme teams want successful implementation and better outcomes. Governance supports sound design, appropriate resourcing, quality, safeguarding, monitoring and timely corrective action.
  • Beneficiaries want quality services, protection, fairness and lasting benefits. Governance helps ensure their interests are considered, their concerns can be raised and programmes remain accountable to them.

An organisation cannot reliably deliver its mission without the capability to manage resources, address risks, stay accountable and make informed decisions. Seen this way, governance is an enabler of each stakeholder's primary objective, not a cost of running the organisation.

4. What we mean by governance, and how it can be measured

Governance is often used loosely. It may help to think of it at four connected levels:

  • Institutional governance: Board effectiveness, strategy, leadership, financial sustainability and organisational risk.
  • Management governance: decision-making, delegation, internal controls, financial management, compliance and performance oversight.
  • Programme governance: design, resource allocation, implementation oversight, safeguarding, monitoring, evaluation and learning.
  • Stakeholder governance: transparency, accountability to beneficiaries, donor stewardship and grievance redressal.

An organisation can be strong at one level and weak at another. It may account for every rupee correctly, yet have no way of knowing whether its programme delivered lasting benefits.

A common objection is that governance cannot be measured. It can, in a structured and repeatable way. One approach is a maturity assessment:

  • Divide governance into a small number of areas, for example Board oversight; risk and continuity; financial stewardship; compliance and ethics; leadership and succession; operations and delivery; digital governance and data protection; and stakeholder trust.
  • Assess each area against specific criteria, answered yes or no on evidence rather than opinion. Does the Board review the risk register every quarter? Is there a documented succession plan? Are grievances recorded and resolved?
  • Score each area on a five-level scale, from vulnerable, through reactive, defined and managed, to optimised.
  • Repeat the assessment each year, so that progress, and the effect of any investment, becomes visible.

Because each answer rests on evidence, two assessors working from the same clear criteria and evidence should reach similar results, and a funder can ask to see that evidence. The same principles can be applied at each stage of a project: criteria for design, implementation, monitoring, closure and post-closure, scored and reported in the same way.

5. Governance across the programme lifecycle

Perhaps the most important contribution of governance is not preventing irregularities or ensuring compliance. It is helping organisations deliver the outcomes they committed to.

Consider a programme that aims to improve the livelihoods of 10,000 people. The organisation completes its activities, uses its approved budget and reports that all 10,000 people received training. But has the intended change happened?

  • Did participants acquire the intended skills?
  • How many found work or improved their livelihoods?
  • Did the benefits continue after the programme ended?
  • Did the programme address the needs originally identified?
  • Were the lessons used in later programmes?

Reaching 10,000 people is an output. Improving their livelihoods is an outcome. Whether those improvements last is a question of sustained impact. Beneficiaries may appear to be served because activities were completed, while the change they needed did not happen. Not every such gap is caused by weak governance: context, design and external events matter too. But sound governance improves an organisation's ability to anticipate, notice, correct and learn from them.

In more than 30 years in finance and governance, much of it in the development sector, I have seen many final reports accepted without anyone asking what changed for the people the programme was meant to serve.

This is why I suggest the five-year test: five years after a project closes, does the benefit still exist for the people it was meant to serve? A project can pass every audit and every final report and still fail this test. Governance at each stage of the lifecycle is what gives a programme a real chance of passing it.

Governance can support each stage:

  1. Design. Are needs properly assessed, objectives realistic and risks understood?
  2. Proposal and resourcing. Are budgets, risks and expected outcomes assessed before commitments are made?
  3. Implementation. Are funds used responsibly, and are quality, procurement, safeguarding and operational risks managed and problems corrected promptly?
  4. Monitoring and evaluation. Are changes in people's lives measured, not just activities and spending?
  5. Closure and transition. Are outcomes assessed, and are assets, responsibilities, knowledge and open issues handed over so benefits can continue?
  6. After closure. Where appropriate, is there a way to learn whether benefits continued three or five years later?

This is where the real return for donors lies. Donors fund programmes to achieve outcomes. With governance built into each stage, they are more likely to get outcomes of quality that last. Without it, they may get high numbers and low quality: activities completed and outputs reported, but change that does not endure.

Two practical suggestions follow. First, governance could be reported at every stage of the project, alongside the programme report and the financial report, not only at audit time. Second, grant agreements could include a clause for a field visit three to five years after completion, to see whether the benefits have continued, with its cost agreed and budgeted at the proposal stage. For many programmes, revisiting a sample of participants may be enough. The effort should be proportionate to the programme's nature, scale and intended duration.

Completing activities and submitting a final report should not be mistaken for lasting impact. Governance keeps organisations accountable not only for what they spend and deliver, but for what they achieve.

6. What happens when governance is under-resourced

Under-investment creates risks well beyond administrative inefficiency: funds exposed to misuse, implementation weaknesses left uncorrected, activities reported without knowing whether outcomes were achieved, beneficiaries exposed to harm or loss of privacy, continuity resting on a few individuals, and donor or regulatory consequences.

These risks are connected. Weak programme monitoring may first look like an operational issue. If it leads to poor outcomes or an inability to show impact, it becomes a funding and reputational issue too, especially for organisations that depend on a few donors.

7. How much should an organisation invest in governance?

Governance spending is usually judged by a ratio: programme-support cost against programme cost. It is worth asking who sets that ratio, and on what basis.

In practice, three parties shape it. Funders set it through grant terms, often as one percentage applied to every partner. Regulators set ceilings for certain kinds of funding. Boards often accept whatever funders allow, without asking whether it is enough.

None of these is designed to establish what a particular organisation actually needs. A uniform percentage cannot reflect differences in size, geography, programme complexity, number of funders and risk. A regulatory ceiling states the maximum permitted, not the amount required. Two organisations with the same ratio can be very differently governed.

This suggests there may be no universally optimum ratio. A more useful approach could be to build the figure from the bottom up, rather than accept it from the top down:

  1. Identify what needs protection: programme outcomes, beneficiary interests, funds, donor confidence, statutory standing and continuity.
  2. Assess current capability: gaps in people, processes, systems, controls, oversight and accountability.
  3. Understand the consequences of leaving gaps open: programme disruption, outcomes not achieved, harm to beneficiaries, financial loss, regulatory or reputational damage.
  4. Determine the investment needed: the people, systems, assurance and management time required to close the priority gaps, in proportion to the organisation's risk.
  5. Check whether it is working: improvements in control effectiveness, programme quality, risk resolution, compliance and sustained outcomes.

The ratio then becomes a result of the analysis, not its starting point. It can be compared with regulatory limits and funder norms. Where there is a gap, the organisation has a reasoned basis for a conversation with its funders, and the Board has a documented basis for its decision. Some investment will be needed regardless of any measurable return, because of statutory duties, fiduciary responsibility or the Board's own risk appetite.

The Board's question becomes: are we investing enough in the capabilities needed to deliver our mission responsibly, manage our risks and sustain our outcomes?

8. What could change: actions for each stakeholder

Responsibility for strengthening governance cannot rest with funders alone. But funding practices strongly shape what organisations prioritise, so funders have a particular role. The suggestions below are starting points, to be adapted to each context.

Sequencing the ask. The right ask depends on where the organisation stands. Project-lifecycle governance cannot work without institutional governance beneath it: a programme cannot be well monitored if the organisation lacks basic financial controls, capable people or Board oversight. So an NGO whose institutional governance is missing or weak could first seek support to build it. An NGO whose institutional governance is already in place, as is often true of those chosen for a grant, can make a more specific request: funding for governance within the project lifecycle, budgeted stage by stage, so that the investment is converted into measurable and lasting outcomes. Either way, the request is stronger when it is tied to what the donor wants to achieve.

Funders could consider:

  • Building governance into each stage of the project lifecycle, from design to post-closure, and budgeting for it in the grant.
  • Asking for governance reporting at each stage, alongside programme and financial reports.
  • Including a grant clause for a field visit three to five years after completion, to see whether the benefits have lasted.
  • In due diligence, asking not only whether a policy, committee or system exists, but whether it is resourced, working and improving.
  • Replacing a uniform administrative percentage with a conversation about the partner's actual needs, based on the analysis in section 7.
  • Treating honest reporting of problems as a sign of good governance, not a reason to withdraw.
  • Supporting institutional capabilities that serve many programmes and donors, such as financial management, HR and IT systems, internal audit and organisational risk management, rather than expecting each grant to fund only the governance directly attributable to it.

NGO CEOs could consider:

  • Assessing first whether institutional governance is in place. If it is missing or weak, seeking support to build it; if it is in place, seeking funding for governance within the project lifecycle.
  • Documenting in each proposal how the governance investment will benefit beneficiaries, and how this will be reported to donors in a measurable and verifiable way. This applies to small and large organisations alike.
  • Presenting governance needs as building on existing strength, to convert it into lasting outcomes, rather than as a deficit.
  • Building outcome measurement and corrective action into programme management, not just activity reporting.
  • Reducing dependence on a few individuals through delegation, documentation and succession planning.

Boards could consider:

  • Assessing governance maturity each year against evidence-based criteria, and tracking the trend.
  • Approving the governance budget on the basis of identified needs, not only on what funders allow.
  • Reviewing programme outcomes and sustainability, not just financial results and activity reports.
  • Applying the five-year test: asking how earlier programmes are doing three to five years after closure, at least for a sample.
  • Tracking whether identified weaknesses are being addressed, and holding management accountable.

Programme leaders could consider:

  • Involving finance, risk and safeguarding colleagues from the design stage, not only at reporting time.
  • Defining realistic outcome indicators and baselines before implementation begins.
  • Planning from the start how benefits will continue after funding ends.
  • Taking joint responsibility with finance and other support functions for programme budgets, risk management, use of resources and achievement of intended outcomes. Governance is not the job of finance, legal or compliance teams alone.

Think tanks and researchers could consider:

  • Piloting governance innovations in the same way the sector pilots thematic and programme innovations: test, measure and share what works.
  • Studying what happens to programme benefits three to five years after closure, and how this relates to governance at each stage.
  • Building the evidence base the sector still lacks, so that the case for governance funding increasingly rests on tested results.

Underlying all of this is one shift: from checking that governance arrangements exist to understanding whether they are working, and what they contribute to outcomes.

9. Governance is an investment in lasting impact

Programmes cannot deliver lasting outcomes without funded governance. The five-year test is where the difference shows.

This is not an argument for unlimited administrative spending, or for putting governance ahead of programmes. It is an argument that programmes and governance cannot be treated as separate investments.

Every programme depends on an institution that can design it well, use resources responsibly, protect beneficiaries, monitor performance, address risks and stay accountable for results. An organisation may complete a project, spend its budget and submit its reports. The real measure of success is whether the intended outcomes were achieved and, where possible, whether the benefits continued after the funding ended.

That needs more than policies and procedures. It needs capable people, effective processes, appropriate systems, meaningful oversight and a culture of accountability. Funders may find that the governance standards they expect cannot be sustained without investment. NGOs, in turn, can show that their governance investments are necessary, proportionate and effective.

The question, then, is not simply how much an organisation spends on governance. It is whether it has invested enough in the capabilities needed to deliver its mission responsibly and sustain its impact. Donors are not simply funding activities or projects. They are investing in institutions entrusted with delivering meaningful and lasting change. The funding they provide must recognise the institutional capabilities required to fulfil that responsibility.
