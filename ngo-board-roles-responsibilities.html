NGO Board Roles and Responsibilities: A Practical Guide

Part of the Institutional Governance & Resilience section of the Knowledge Hub.

By CA Sunil Kumar, FCA · 17 November 2026 · 4 min read

An NGO Board is not an honorary title. Whatever the law you are registered under (a trust, a society, a company limited by guarantee, an NGO or public benefit organisation), the Board is where accountability sits. When something goes wrong, donors, regulators and the public look to the Board first.

This page uses the words NGO boards use in Kenya, Nigeria, Uganda, South Africa, India and across the development sector. The same duties apply to a nonprofit board in the US or charity trustees in the UK.

General guidance for governance and education. Your registration law, constitution and donor agreements come first.

The core duties of every Board member

DutyWhat it means in practice
Act in the organisation's interestDecide for the mission and the people it serves, not for yourself, a donor, a political group or a family member.
Care and diligenceRead the papers, attend, ask questions, and follow up when an answer is unclear.
Stay within the law and the constitutionKnow what the organisation is registered to do and the rules on foreign funding, tax, labour and reporting that apply to it.
Protect the assetsMake sure money, property, data and reputation are used for the purposes they were given for.
Avoid and declare conflictsDeclare any interest, leave the room when it is discussed, and keep a register.
Be accountableMake sure accounts are audited, reports are filed on time and donors and members get honest information.

What the Board does, and what it leaves to management

The BoardManagement (CEO or executive director and staff)
Approves mission, strategy and annual budgetProposes the strategy and budget and delivers them
Appoints, supports and appraises the CEO, and sets their payRecruits, manages and appraises the rest of the staff
Approves key policies: finance, conflict of interest, safeguarding, whistleblowing, reservesWrites procedures under those policies and applies them
Oversees the main risks and asks what is being done about themKeeps the risk register and manages the risks
Receives the audit and meets the auditorPrepares the accounts and fixes audit findings
Approves large contracts, borrowing, property and new country or programme entriesSigns and manages contracts within delegated limits

A written delegation of authority, one or two pages saying who may approve what and up to which amount, prevents most Board and CEO disputes. Donors often ask to see it.

The office-bearers

  • Chair. Leads the Board, sets agendas with the CEO, makes sure every voice is heard, and is the CEO's main point of contact between meetings. The chair does not manage staff.
  • Treasurer or finance committee chair. Helps the Board understand the finances: cash, reserves, budget against actual, audit findings. The treasurer does not keep the books or sign every cheque.
  • Secretary. Makes sure meetings are properly called, minutes are accurate and approved, and statutory filings and registers are up to date. In many NGOs a staff member does the administration, but a Board member owns it.
  • Committees. Finance and audit, programme, and nominations or governance are common. Committees prepare decisions; the full Board takes them.

Common problems in NGO Boards

  • A founder who chairs the Board and runs the organisation, with no one to challenge them.
  • Board members who are friends, relatives or staff of the CEO.
  • Meetings that only receive reports and never take decisions.
  • No fixed terms, so the same members serve for decades.
  • Finance papers that the Board does not understand but approves anyway.
  • The Board hears about problems first from a donor or the media.

Donors look for exactly these signs in a pre-award or capacity assessment. Fixing them early is cheaper than explaining them later.

One-page Board checklist

  1. Every Board member has a copy of the constitution and knows what the organisation is registered to do.
  2. Members have fixed terms and there is a plan for renewing the Board.
  3. The Board met at least four times last year, with quorum, and the minutes record decisions.
  4. There is a written delegation of authority between the Board and the CEO.
  5. The CEO was appraised by the Board in the last 12 months.
  6. Conflicts of interest are declared at every meeting and recorded in a register.
  7. The Board approved this year's budget and sees budget against actual at every meeting.
  8. The Board reviewed the main risks in the last six months.
  9. The audited accounts were received and discussed by the Board, with the auditor present or available.
  10. Statutory returns and donor reports were filed on time.

If several of these are missing, start with a short Board self-assessment to agree what to fix first.

Questions people ask

What is the main role of an NGO Board?

The Board is accountable for the organisation. It sets direction, appoints and oversees the CEO or executive director, makes sure money is used properly and lawfully, and protects the organisation's reputation and future. It governs; management runs the day-to-day work.

What is the difference between the Board and management in an NGO?

The Board decides what the organisation is trying to achieve and checks that it is happening. Management decides how to do it and does it. A useful test: if a decision needs to be made this week to keep programmes running, it is usually management's; if it changes direction, risk or accountability, it is usually the Board's.

Can NGO Board members be paid?

It depends on the law you are registered under and your constitution. Many countries and donors expect Board members of charities and NGOs to be unpaid apart from reasonable expenses, and where payment is allowed it should be approved openly and recorded. Check your registration law and governing document.

Are NGO Board members personally liable?

Board members can face personal consequences if they act dishonestly, ignore clear warning signs or allow the organisation to break the law. Acting in good faith, asking questions, recording decisions and declaring conflicts are the best protection.
